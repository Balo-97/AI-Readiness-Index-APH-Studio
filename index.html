AI Readiness Index · Analytic Hierarchy Process

How ready is each neighbourhood for AI?

The ARI Weighting Studio lets a panel of local experts weigh what matters for AI readiness, then scores every census tract or county on Engagement Opportunity, the ways AI reaches residents, and on their Adoption Potential, their ability to use it.

Open the studio Download the Charlotte example Map another city View on GitHub

Runs entirely in your browser: nothing to install, and your data stays on your computer.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence is reaching many areas of community life at once, from transport and health care to education and work. It will not arrive the same way everywhere. Whether residents can benefit depends on whether they have the digital infrastructure AI runs on, and the education, income and skills to use it. Both vary sharply between neighbourhoods within the same city, yet existing AI-readiness indices compare whole countries.

The AI Readiness Index (ARI) measures readiness at the census tract or county level, with two pillars:

EO Engagement Opportunity

The ways AI reaches residents: the digital infrastructure it runs on, work in jobs where AI is already used, and services such as health care and transport that use AI on residents' behalf.

AP Adoption Potential

Residents' ability to make use of it: education, income, employment and skills.

Census tracts show the differences between neighbourhoods within a city; counties compare places across a region. The studio switches between the two with the Unit of analysis setting on the Index tab, using the same expert weights for both.

How much each indicator should count is a value judgement that local policy makers and experts have to make for their own area. The studio helps a panel make those judgements in a structured, transparent and checkable way with the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). It then turns them into weights, scores every tract or county, maps the results and tests how stable they are. It is tested on Charlotte and Mecklenburg County, North Carolina, with data from the Carolinas Regional Explorer and the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey.

Methodology

The ARI combines two streams. Experts' pairwise judgements become indicator weights; each indicator's raw value for every tract becomes a score between 0 and 1. The two meet in the pillar scores. The worked example after the formulas follows one tract through every step.

1. Hierarchy. The goal, the AI readiness of census tracts or counties, splits into dimensions (Level 1), and each dimension into indicators (Level 2). Each indicator belongs to one pillar, Engagement Opportunity (EO) or Adoption Potential (AP), and has a direction: higher is better, or lower is better.
2. Pairwise comparisons. For each pair of siblings, an expert answers how much more important one is than the other: 1 equal, 3 moderately, 5 strongly, 7 very strongly, 9 extremely more important, with 2, 4, 6 and 8 in between. The answers fill a reciprocal matrix with \(a_{ii}=1\) and \(a_{ji}=1/a_{ij}\).
3. Local weights. The row geometric mean gives the weights within each set: $$g_i=\Bigl(\prod_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}\Bigr)^{1/n},\qquad w_i=\frac{g_i}{\sum_k g_k}.$$
4. Consistency. With \(\lambda_{\max}\) the principal eigenvalue of the matrix, $$\mathrm{CI}=\frac{\lambda_{\max}-n}{n-1},\qquad \mathrm{CR}=\frac{\mathrm{CI}}{\mathrm{RI}(n)},$$ where \(\mathrm{RI}\) is Saaty's random index (0.58 for three items, 0.90 for four). Sets with \(\mathrm{CR}<0.10\) are accepted; above that the studio points to the judgements to revisit.
5. Combining the group. A team can agree on each judgement by consensus and enter one set of matrices, or let every member judge separately and pool the answers. In the second case each cell is pooled across the \(K\) experts by a weighted geometric mean, which keeps the matrix reciprocal: $$a^{G}_{ij}=\prod_{k=1}^{K}a_{ij,k}^{\,\lambda_k/\sum_l\lambda_l}.$$ Pooling hides disagreement: if one member answers 5 and another 1/5, the pooled value is 1, a view that neither holds. To work by consensus, add one participant (for example “Group”), enter the agreed judgements under that name, and untick everyone else under Include on the Group weights tab.
6. Global and pillar weights. An indicator's global weight is its dimension's weight times its weight within the dimension, \(w_j=w_d\,w_{j\mid d}\). Weights are then rescaled to sum to 1 within each pillar: \(w^{P}_j=w_j/\sum_{k\in P}w_k\).
7. Indicator scores. Tracts with fewer residents than a minimum (100 by default) are left unscored first: survey estimates for a handful of residents are mostly 0% or 100% and would otherwise set the scale for everyone else. Each indicator is then rescaled across the \(N\) remaining tracts of the study area, flipped if lower is better, and raised off zero by a small floor \(f=0.01\): $$z_{tj}=\frac{x_{tj}-\min_s x_{sj}}{\max_s x_{sj}-\min_s x_{sj}},\qquad x'_{tj}=f+(1-f)\,z_{tj}.$$
8. Pillar scores. A weighted geometric mean on a 0–100 scale, so a weak indicator cannot be fully offset by a strong one: $$\mathit{EO}_t=100\prod_{j\in \mathit{EO}}\bigl(x'_{tj}\bigr)^{w^{\mathit{EO}}_j},\qquad \mathit{AP}_t=100\prod_{j\in \mathit{AP}}\bigl(x'_{tj}\bigr)^{w^{\mathit{AP}}_j}.$$
9. Index, gap and gap labels. $$\mathrm{ARI}_t=\sqrt{\mathit{EO}_t\,\mathit{AP}_t}.$$ The ARI is high only when both pillars are high. The readiness gap compares the two pillars, in one of three versions: $$G^{z}_t=\frac{\mathit{EO}_t-\overline{\mathit{EO}}}{s_{\mathit{EO}}}-\frac{\mathit{AP}_t-\overline{\mathit{AP}}}{s_{\mathit{AP}}}\ \text{(standardised, default)},\qquad G^{\%}_t=\mathrm{pct}(\mathit{EO}_t)-\mathrm{pct}(\mathit{AP}_t),\qquad G^{\mathrm{raw}}_t=\mathit{EO}_t-\mathit{AP}_t,$$ where \(\mathrm{pct}\) is the percentile rank in the study area. Min–max gives each indicator its own ruler, so the raw gap mostly reflects how the indicators are spread: in the Charlotte example, a tract at the median on every indicator has a raw gap of about +34. The standardised and percentile-rank gaps compare each tract with the rest of the study area. A positive gap means a tract stands out more on engagement opportunity than on adoption potential.

A negative gap can simply mean adoption potential is exceptional, not that engagement opportunity is lacking, so each tract also gets a gap label from the terciles of EO and AP (low = bottom third of the study area):

10. Robustness and spatial pattern. A Monte Carlo analysis moves every judgement at random along the scale, recomputes everything, and reports each tract's range of ranks. Moran's I and local Moran (LISA) statistics, with a false-discovery-rate correction, show where high and low readiness cluster. They use 9,999 permutations by default: with \(n\) tracts the correction can only find a single tract significant if the number of permutations is at least \(n/\alpha-1\) (about 6,000 for 300 tracts), and the studio warns when it is lower.
Responsible use. Weights are value judgements. The same flexibility that lets a community set its own priorities could be used to make an area look more or less ready than it is. Document who took part and what they judged, report consistency, run the sensitivity analysis, and publish the weights with the results.

Pairwise judgements

Saaty 1–9 scale, two levels

Each team member compares two items at a time: the dimensions first (Level 1), then the indicators within each dimension (Level 2). The mirror cell is the reciprocal (3 → 1/3).

2

Local weights

Each matrix → weights

The row geometric mean of each matrix gives dimension weights \(w_d\) (Level 1) and local weights \(w_{j|d}\) within each dimension (Level 2). Every matrix is checked for consistency.

The global weights sum to 1. They are then rescaled to sum to 1 within each pillar, so broadband's 0.18 becomes 0.36 of Engagement Opportunity.

Quick start with the Charlotte example

  1. Open the studio. Open it here. On a first visit it shows a demo; nothing in the demo is saved.
  2. Load the example project. Download the Charlotte example, then in the studio click Import project file… in the banner (or Setup → Import .json files…, or drag the file onto the page). Check the preview and press Start the project.
  3. Add yourself. In the top bar choose You are → ＋ New participant… and type your name.
  4. Compare. On the Compare tab, click the number on the side of the more important item for each pair (16 pairs in this example). Revise any set whose consistency ratio is above 0.10.
  5. See the weights on the Group weights tab.
  6. Compute the index. On the Index tab click Load example tracts (CRXP + Census ACS, 2024). The study area is already Mecklenburg County. Until every pair is answered, equal weights stand in and results are marked provisional.
  7. Explore. Map the ARI, the pillars, the gap labels, the gap or the bivariate EO × AP classes; click a tract for its breakdown; run the sensitivity analysis; look for spatial clusters.
  8. Save. Setup → Export project + all judgements (.json) keeps your work; Export all… saves every output as one zip.

Working as a team

This version keeps everything in your browser, so a team combines its work by exchanging files:

  1. The facilitator builds the hierarchy on the Setup tab, agrees it with the group, clicks Lock hierarchy, and sends everyone Export project + all judgements (.json).
  2. Each member imports that file, adds themselves as a participant, makes their comparisons, and sends back Export only my judgements.
  3. The facilitator imports all the returned files at once with Group weights → Import teammates' files… or by dragging them onto the page. A preview shows each person and what will change; nothing is saved until you press Import.

Everyone must judge the same hierarchy: judgements made on different dimensions or indicators cannot be pooled, and the preview warns you when that happens.

Using your own data

The table (CSV)

Any CSV with a header row and one row per area works: tracts, block groups, neighbourhoods or counties. For county-level data, switch Unit of analysis on the Index tab to Counties before you upload; the census-tract and county levels each keep their own table and map.

  • An ID column, such as geoid, unique for each row. It also joins the table to the map.
  • A name column (optional), such as tract_name.
  • A column that defines the study area (optional), such as county_name.
  • A population column (recommended), such as total-population, so areas with almost no residents can be left out.
  • One numeric column per indicator: percentages, rates, medians or counts.
  • Reliability columns (optional): a column named <indicator>_cv is read as that indicator's coefficient of variation (%), and estimates above 30% are flagged as unreliable.

The template shows the layout:

geoid,tract_name,county_name,household-income,household-income_cv,acs-broadband-any,...
37119000101,Second Ward / Uptown,Mecklenburg County,119044.0,10.01,97.1429,...

How values are read. Columns may be separated by commas, semicolons or tabs, and quoted fields may contain commas. Thousands separators, $, % and spaces are ignored, so $61,250 and 12.5% read as numbers. Blank cells and NA, N/A, null, NaN, none and - count as missing. Decimal commas (1,5) are not supported.

  1. On the Index tab, click Upload CSV… and choose your file.
  2. Set the ID column, the name column and, for a subset, Study area: filter rows by a column and its values. Check the Population column and Minimum residents (default 100): areas with fewer residents are left unscored and cannot set any indicator's minimum or maximum. A column named like total-population is found on its own; choose None or a minimum of 0 to switch the rule off.
  3. On the Setup tab, give each indicator its data column, its pillar and its direction.
  4. On the Index tab, choose how missing values are handled (leave the area unscored, reweight, or fill with the median) and the normalisation (min–max or percentile).

The boundaries (GeoJSON)

The built-in map covers only the 14 counties of the Charlotte region. For anywhere else, click Upload boundaries (GeoJSON)… on the Index tab. The file needs polygons or multipolygons, one property whose values match your table's ID column (the studio finds it, and you can change it under the map's Join setting), and longitude/latitude coordinates (WGS 84). A shapefile or GeoPackage converts to GeoJSON in QGIS or at mapshaper.org, which can also simplify large files so they draw faster.

Getting a map for another US city

Census tract boundaries for every state are free from the U.S. Census Bureau. It takes about five minutes.

  1. Download the tracts for your state. Open the Census Bureau's Cartographic Boundary Files, pick the year that matches your data, and download Census Tracts for your state as a shapefile (.zip). For North Carolina in 2024 the file is cb_2024_37_tract_500k.zip. Tract boundaries are fixed for the decade, so any year from 2020 on matches current ACS estimates.
  2. Cut it to your area and convert it at mapshaper.org, a free tool that runs in your browser.
    • Drag the .zip onto the page and click Import.
    • To keep only your county, click Console, type a filter with the county's three-digit code and press Enter. For Mecklenburg County (code 119): 
      -filter 'COUNTYFP == "119"'
      For several counties: -filter '["119","025","179"].includes(COUNTYFP)'. The county code is digits 3–5 of any tract's GEOID.
    • Click Simplify and set the slider to about 10–20% so the map draws quickly.
    • Click Export, choose GeoJSON and save the file.
  3. Upload it in the studio. On the Index tab, click Upload boundaries (GeoJSON)…. The studio matches the file's GEOID to your table's ID column on its own. Check that the map's Join setting shows every row matched; rows without a shape have IDs that are not in the file.
  4. Optional: add an outline. County outlines help orient readers. Download Counties from the same Census page, convert it the same way, and add it under Outline layer → Upload….

Working at the county level? Switch Unit of analysis on the Index tab to Counties first; each level keeps its own table and map. In step 1, download Counties instead of Census Tracts. That file covers the whole country, so filter it to your state or region in step 2, for example -filter 'STATEFP == "37"' for North Carolina. A county's GEOID has 5 digits: state (2) and county (3), for example 37119.

Make the IDs in your table match. A tract's GEOID has 11 digits: state (2), county (3) and tract (6), for example 37119000101.

  • data.census.gov tables have a GEO_ID column such as 1400000US37119000101. Keep only the last 11 digits (in Excel, =RIGHT(A2,11)).
  • The Census API returns separate state, county and tract columns. Join them in that order.
  • Excel removes leading zeros, so Alabama's 01073000100 becomes 1073000100. The studio ignores leading zeros when matching, so either form works.

Other places and other kinds of area

Any polygon boundaries work, as long as one property matches an ID column in your table. Examples include block groups (from the same Census page), neighbourhoods, wards or council districts from a city's open-data portal, and small statistical areas from a national statistics office outside the US. City portals often publish shapefiles in a local projection. Convert those to longitude/latitude before exporting: type -proj wgs84 in the mapshaper console, or in QGIS choose CRS EPSG:4326 when you save as GeoJSON.

Privacy. Your tables and boundaries are processed in your browser and never sent anywhere. They are kept in this browser for next time; use the Export buttons to keep copies.

Running it locally

One file, no installation

Download ARI_Weighting_Studio.html and double-click it. It opens in any modern browser and works offline, with the Charlotte example data built in.

The whole repository

git clone https://github.com/Balo-97/AI-Readiness-Index-APH-Studio.git
cd AI-Readiness-Index-APH-Studio
python -m http.server 8000

Then open http://localhost:8000/.

Changing the code or the built-in data

The studio's source is app/ari_weighting_studio.html. After editing it, or replacing the files in data/, rebuild the standalone file (Python 3.8 or later, no extra packages):

python tools/build_standalone.py

To regenerate the example project, run python tools/make_four_dimension_example.py. The source file is a page body without the surrounding document, so open the built ARI_Weighting_Studio.html.
